Scope & Conventions

Scope
US / UK / Commonwealth + ANZAC (and ABDA elements where Anglo-American-Australian ships shared the engagement). Naval, air, and the land phases of Pacific amphibious operations included per agreed scoping. Excludes CBI / Burma (held for List 4), Soviet operations against Japan (Manchuria, Sakhalin), and Chinese forces.
Period
December 1941 – September 1945.
Sort
Start date; range shown in entry. Multi-month campaigns bracketed at their first major action.
Codenames
Allied codenames in entry titles; Japanese codenames italicized in parens where attested (e.g. Shō-Gō 1, MO Sakusen, Ten-Gō). Note that Japanese ground/naval ops often known by Sakusen (作戦, operation) plus designation.

About This Reference

↑ Top
FrameworkThis is the Pacific volume of Heritage Fine Scale's operational reference: a chronological index of the naval, air, and amphibious land operations of the Pacific and South-West Pacific, from Pearl Harbor in December 1941 to the surrender in Tokyo Bay in September 1945. It is a reference, not a narrative: entries are sorted by start date and stated in the terms an operation was planned and judged in.
StructureEntries are grouped by calendar year and ordered by date of first major action; multi-year efforts are bracketed at their opening as campaign or program brackets. Each entry follows the same three-field anatomy (Forces, Intent, Outcome), the skeleton the dossiers use, so the reference and the long-form briefs read in one register. Allied codenames carry the entry title; Axis codenames are set in italic where the scholarship attests them.
DoctrineInclusion is by consequence, not by drama: an action earns an entry if it changed the shape of the theater, whether or not it is famous. The through-line is the house argument, the gap between what gets remembered and what actually mattered, applied at theater scale. Treatment is evenhanded; Axis intent and outcome are stated as the Axis command understood them. Scope edges are deliberate: operations outside this theater appear only where they touch it directly.
Operational OutcomesRead in order, the theater resolves into a recognizable shape. 1941–42 is Japanese expansion and a run of Allied catastrophes (the Philippines, Malaya, the East Indies) checked at the Coral Sea and broken at Midway, the hinge of the whole war. 1942–43 is the attritional grind of Guadalcanal and New Guinea, where the initiative changed hands for good. 1943–44 is the two-axis advance, the Central Pacific island chain and MacArthur's South-West Pacific drive, converging on the Marianas and Leyte, where the Imperial Navy was destroyed as a fighting force. 1945 is the close at Iwo Jima and Okinawa, the bombing and blockade of the Home Islands, and the surrender. For the modeler, that arc places a subject in the war rather than on a shelf.

1941

↑ Top

7 December 1941

Attack on Pearl Harbor (Axis: Hawai Sakusen)

ForcesUS Pacific Fleet (Kimmel) at anchor: 8 BBs, 8 cruisers, 30 destroyers; Army and Navy air on Oahu; vs IJN 1st Air Fleet (Nagumo, under Yamamoto): 6 fleet carriers, 353 aircraft in two waves; auxiliary midget submarine force.
Intent (Japanese)Cripple the US battle line in a surprise strike to allow freedom of action for the Nanshin (southward advance) into the Southern Resource Area before US power could intervene.
Outcome4 US BBs sunk (Arizona, Oklahoma, California, West Virginia), 4 damaged; 188 aircraft destroyed; ~2,400 Americans killed. Critically, the three US carriers (Enterprise, Lexington, Saratoga) were at sea, and the fuel reserves and dry docks were untouched: the strategic miss that mattered most. Tactical Japanese success, strategic disaster; mobilized US opinion irreversibly.

7 December 1941 – August 1945

USN Submarine Campaign Against Japan [Bracket]

ForcesPacific Fleet Submarines (ComSubPac, Lockwood from February 1943) from Pearl Harbor, Midway, later Saipan and Guam; SoWesPac submarines (Christie, then Fife) from Brisbane and Fremantle; ~250 fleet boats (Gato, Balao, Tench classes) built during the war; supported by FRUPac and FRUMel signals intelligence routing Japanese convoys via JN-25 and maru code decryption. Notable aces: Morton (Wahoo), O'Kane (Tang), Dealey (Harder), Fluckey (Barb).
IntentFrom the hour of Pearl Harbor (the Chief of Naval Operations' order "Execute unrestricted air and submarine warfare against Japan" went out at 1752 Washington time, 7 Dec 1941): sever the Japanese empire from its captured Southern Resource Area, particularly the oil, bauxite, tin, and rubber LOCs from the East Indies to the home islands.
OutcomeStrategically arguably as decisive as the carrier war. Hampered through 1942 by the Mk 14 torpedo's chronic defects (faulty magnetic exploder, depth-keeping ~10 ft below set, unreliable contact pistol on perpendicular impact), a problem the Bureau of Ordnance resisted acknowledging until Lockwood and Nimitz forced the issue in summer 1943. Once corrected: ~1,300 Japanese merchantmen sunk (~5M GRT, ~55% of total Japanese maritime losses) and ~200 naval combatants including 8 carriers (notably Shōkaku, Taihō, and Shinano, the latter sunk by Archerfish on her maiden voyage, 29 Nov 1944, the largest warship ever sunk by submarine), BB Kongō (sunk by Sealion II Nov 1944), 11 cruisers, ~38 destroyers, 23 Japanese submarines. By 1945 Japanese tanker tonnage was reduced to ~10% of 1942 levels. Combined with B-29 aerial mining (Operation Starvation, March–August 1945), which closed the Shimonoseki Strait, brought Japanese industry to a standstill regardless of the projected invasion. Cost: 52 submarines lost (one in five committed), ~3,500 submariners killed: the highest fatality rate of any US service branch in the war.

7 December 1941 – 2 September 1945

Pacific SIGINT: FRUPac / Station HYPO / FRUMel [Program Bracket]

ForcesFleet Radio Unit Pacific (FRUPac, Station HYPO) at Pearl Harbor under Joseph J. Rochefort to October 1942, then John R. Redman, then Joseph N. Wenger; Fleet Radio Unit Melbourne (FRUMel) under Rudolph J. Fabian; Cast/Cavite/Belconnen (Philippines, evacuated to Australia February 1942); supporting OP-20-G in Washington; Combat Intelligence Unit (Layton on Nimitz's staff translating SIGINT into operational intelligence); from 1943, US–UK cooperation through HMS Anderson (Colombo) and US Army's Central Bureau (Brisbane, Akin). Primary target: JN-25 series, kaigun ango-sho D, the Japanese Navy general operational cipher; secondary: Maru code (merchant shipping movements), J-19 / Purple (Foreign Ministry, principally a State Department / Arlington Hall product), aviation and weather codes.
IntentRead Japanese naval operational and merchant traffic in time to act; route US carrier task forces and submarine wolfpacks against intercepted Japanese movements; protect the source through compartmented distribution and operational cover ("scouting" reports, search-plane "luck").
OutcomeStrategically decisive across the Pacific war. Operational milestones: the "AF water shortage" deception that confirmed Midway as the target of MI Sakusen (May 1942, Rochefort's gambit that established the target identifier); Coral Sea, Midway, Eastern Solomons, Santa Cruz, and Philippine Sea engagements all turned on advance knowledge of Japanese carrier movements; Operation Vengeance (April 1943, Yamamoto's killing) was a SIGINT product; the submarine campaign's catastrophic effect on Japanese merchant tonnage (Atlantic-comparable strategic weight, ~55% of total Japanese merchant losses by US submarines) depended on FRUPac routing of US boats onto convoys whose schedules they had read days in advance. Cryptographic gaps were brief: JN-25(b), closed by version-roll in late 1942, was back within weeks. Rochefort's reassignment from HYPO in October 1942, at the moment of his greatest contribution to Midway, remains the institutional scandal of US wartime cryptography; partly redressed posthumously when his analytical role was formally recognized in the 1980s. Comparable in strategic weight to Ultra in the Atlantic (L1); the two efforts ran in deliberate cooperation from 1943 onward.

8–25 December 1941

Battle of Hong Kong

ForcesUK garrison (Maltby): Royal Scots, Middlesex, 2/14 Punjab, 5/7 Rajput; 2 Cdn battalions (Winnipeg Grenadiers, Royal Rifles of Canada, "C Force"); HKVDC; vs Japanese 38th Division (Sano).
IntentHold Hong Kong as long as possible to deny it to Japan and demonstrate British resolve in Asia; reinforcement plans depended on a Singapore-led counter-offensive that never materialized.
OutcomeGin Drinkers' Line broken by 13 Dec; defenders withdrew to Hong Kong Island and were progressively reduced. Surrender on Christmas Day. ~1,560 Allied dead, ~9,500 POWs, many later murdered, starved, or worked to death in Japanese captivity. First Canadian Army combat of the war; first surrender of British territory in the Pacific.

8–23 December 1941

Battle of Wake Island

Forces1st Defense Battalion USMC (Devereux): ~450 Marines + 1,200 civilian construction workers; VMF-211 (12 F4F Wildcats); vs Japanese South Seas Force (Kajioka).
Intent (Allied)Hold the central Pacific atoll as a forward outpost; deny Japan a base on the Hawaii route.
OutcomeFirst Japanese landing attempt (11 Dec) repulsed: destroyers Hayate and Kisaragi sunk, the first IJN surface losses of the war. Relief force (TF 14 built around Saratoga) recalled by Pye on 22 Dec: the war's first command-failure-of-nerve controversy. Second Japanese assault overwhelmed the garrison on 23 Dec. ~122 US dead, ~1,600 POWs (98 civilians later massacred by the Japanese garrison in October 1943).

8 December 1941 – 15 February 1942

Battle of Malaya / Fall of Singapore

ForcesMalaya Command (Percival): III Indian Corps + AIF 8th Div + UK formations, ~88,000; Force Z (Phillips): HMS Prince of Wales, Repulse; vs Japanese 25th Army (Yamashita) ~70,000 + 22nd Air Flotilla.
Intent (Allied)Defend Malaya and the Singapore Naval Base; preserve "Fortress Singapore" as the linchpin of British Far East strategy.
OutcomeCatastrophe. Force Z destroyed by land-based torpedo aircraft off Kuantan, 10 Dec: the first capital ships ever sunk underway by air alone, ending the battleship era in real terms. Yamashita's bicycle-mobile infantry advanced ~600 miles down the peninsula in 70 days, repeatedly outflanking prepared positions. Singapore surrendered 15 Feb: ~80,000 POWs, the largest British capitulation in history. Churchill: "the worst disaster and largest capitulation in British history."

8 December 1941 – 8 May 1942

Philippines Campaign (1941–42)

ForcesUS Army Forces Far East (MacArthur, then Wainwright): US/Filipino troops ~150,000; supported by Asiatic Fleet (Hart); vs Japanese 14th Army (Homma).
Intent (Allied)Defend the Philippines per the late-revised War Plan ORANGE / Rainbow Five derivatives; hold Bataan–Corregidor until reinforcement, which never came, given the Europe-first ratification.
OutcomeManila evacuated 26 Dec; Bataan defense (Jan–April) held longer than Japanese projections. MacArthur evacuated to Australia 11 March on Roosevelt's orders ("I shall return"). Bataan surrendered 9 April, followed by the Bataan Death March (~5,000–10,000 deaths in transit). Corregidor fell 6 May. ~23,000 US/Filipino casualties; ~75,000 POWs. Ratified Japanese command of the Western Pacific through 1942.

27 February – 1 March 1942

Battle of the Java Sea (and ABDA Collapse)

ForcesCombined ABDA Strike Force (Doorman, RNN): HNLMS De Ruyter, Java, Kortenaer, Witte de With; HMS Exeter, Encounter, Jupiter, Electra; HMAS Perth; USS Houston; vs Japanese Eastern Invasion Force escort (Takagi).
Intent (Allied)Disrupt the Japanese invasion convoy bound for Java; hold the Malay Barrier as the last coherent Allied position in the Indies.
OutcomeTactical annihilation in seven hours of running engagement and pursuit. Doorman killed aboard De Ruyter; Java, Kortenaer, Electra, Jupiter sunk. Exeter sunk in Second Java Sea (1 March); Perth and Houston destroyed at Sunda Strait the same night. ABDA Command dissolved 25 Feb; Java surrendered 9 March. Last unified Allied attempt to hold the Indies.

8 December 1941 – 2 September 1945

Japanese Treatment of Prisoners and Occupied Populations [Bracket]

ForcesImperial General Headquarters policy directives (Daihon'ei); Kempeitai (military police) under Army administration; the IJN's Special Naval Landing Forces and SNLF garrisons in island and city pacification roles; civilian-administered concentration and labor camps under regional military commands; medical units including Unit 731 (Tōgō Butai) at Pingfang, Manchuria, under Ishii Shirō; comfort women administration through the Army Welfare Section; conscripted labor from Korea (chōyō), China, and occupied territories.
IntentImperial military policy held Allied POWs in contempt as men who had dishonored themselves by surrender: the inverse of Bushidō's gyokusai ("jeweled-shattering") ideal, which became compulsory for Japanese forces in extremis. Civilian populations of occupied territories were treated under varying regional regimes ranging from co-optation (the Dai-Tōa Kyōeiken, Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere construct) to systematic abuse (Korea, occupied China, the Philippines under Iwabuchi, Manchurian and Northeast Asian populations subject to Unit 731's experimentation).
OutcomeAllied POW death rate ~27% in Japanese custody versus ~4% in German custody: the empirical scale of the disparity. The Bataan Death March (April 1942, ~5,000–10,000 dead in transit, covered in the Philippines 1941–42 entry above) was emblematic, not anomalous. Sandakan death marches (Borneo, January–August 1945): ~2,400 Australian and British POWs dead, six survivors. "Hellships" transporting POWs across the Pacific without marking, many sunk by US submarines and aircraft unaware of the cargo (~21,000 POW dead in hellship sinkings: the Arisan Maru, Junyō Maru, Ōryoku Maru among the worst). Comfort women system (ianfu): ~50,000–200,000 women (primarily Korean and Chinese, also Filipina, Indonesian, Dutch) coerced into military brothels (ianjo) across the occupied territories; the historiographical and political weight of this issue remains live in 2026 diplomacy. Forced labor: ~60,000 Allied POWs and ~200,000 conscripted Asian laborers (rōmusha) on the Thailand–Burma Railway: ~12,000 POW and ~90,000 rōmusha dead; Hashima/Gunkanjima coal works; Mitsubishi shipyards. Manila Massacre (3 February – 3 March 1945, ~100,000 Filipino civilians killed by SNLF troops under Iwabuchi defying Yamashita's stated intention of orderly withdrawal, referenced in the Philippines 1944–45 bracket). Unit 731: Biological warfare program conducting human experimentation on Chinese, Korean, Russian, and Allied POW subjects; ~3,000 direct experimental victims, with field-trial dispersals (anthrax, plague, cholera) across Manchuria and northern China killing further tens of thousands. The Tokyo war crimes tribunal (IMTFE, 1946–48) addressed a subset of this material; the Unit 731 leadership was granted immunity by US intelligence in exchange for research data, a postwar accommodation that remains historically contested. Operational intersection with this list: the Doolittle Raid's coastal-China reprisals (Zhejiang–Jiangxi, summer 1942, ~250,000 dead) and the IJA's Sankō Sakusen ("three-alls" policy: 殺光、燒光、搶光, kill all, burn all, loot all) in occupied China are the two clearest cases where this treatment regime affected the operational tempo of Allied campaigns.

18 April 1942

Doolittle Raid

Forces16 USAAF B-25B Mitchells (Doolittle); launched from USS Hornet (Mitscher, TF 16 under Halsey); 80 aircrew.
IntentOne-way bombing raid on Tokyo, Yokohama, Yokosuka, Nagoya, Kobe, and Osaka; recover crews in unoccupied China. Symbolic strike on the home islands and morale boost after the cascade of disasters.
OutcomeMinimal physical damage. 15 B-25s crashed in China after fuel exhaustion; one landed in the USSR (crew interned). 3 aircrew killed in crashes, 8 captured by Japanese (3 later executed). Japanese reprisals in coastal China killed an estimated 250,000 civilians during the Zhejiang–Jiangxi operation. Strategically pivotal: drove Yamamoto's commitment to the Midway operation as the means to force decisive battle.

4–8 May 1942

Battle of the Coral Sea (Axis: MO Sakusen)

ForcesTF 17 (Fletcher): USS Lexington, Yorktown; ANZAC Squadron (Crace): HMAS Australia, Hobart + USS Chicago; vs MO Striking Force (Takagi/Hara): Shōkaku, Zuikaku, light carrier Shōhō.
Intent (Japanese)Capture Port Moresby by amphibious assault; isolate Australia. (Allied): Block the landing through carrier interdiction.
OutcomeFirst carrier-vs-carrier battle in history; the opposing surface forces never sighted each other. Lexington sunk; Yorktown damaged. Shōhō sunk; Shōkaku damaged; Zuikaku's air group savaged. Tactical Japanese victory in tonnage but strategic Allied victory: Port Moresby operation postponed, then abandoned. Critically, both Shōkaku and Zuikaku missed Midway: arguably the decisive contribution to that battle's outcome.

4–7 June 1942

Battle of Midway (Axis: MI Sakusen)

ForcesUS Pacific Fleet (Nimitz, via Spruance and Fletcher): TF 16 (Enterprise, Hornet), TF 17 (Yorktown); vs Combined Fleet 1st Mobile Force (Nagumo, under Yamamoto's overall command): Akagi, Kaga, Sōryū, Hiryū.
Intent (Japanese)Lure the US carriers to defend Midway atoll; achieve decisive engagement and complete the work begun at Pearl Harbor. (Allied): Ambush the strike force using Station HYPO's break of JN-25(b).
OutcomeAll four Japanese carriers sunk on 4 June: the famous five minutes when McClusky's and Leslie's SBDs caught Akagi, Kaga, Sōryū with armed and fueled aircraft on deck. Yorktown lost to Hiryū's counter-strike. ~3,000 Japanese dead including the irreplaceable cadre of Nagumo's air groups; ~300 US. Strategic turning point at sea; restored carrier parity then shifted decisively to US advantage.

21 July – 16 November 1942

Kokoda Track Campaign

ForcesAUS 39th Bn militia (Maroubra Force), 53rd Bn, then AIF 7th Div (Vasey); PNG native carriers ("Fuzzy Wuzzy Angels"); vs Japanese South Seas Detachment (Horii).
IntentHold the Owen Stanley Range against the Japanese overland push from Buna toward Port Moresby; deny Japan the ability to interdict US–Australia sea LOCs.
OutcomeBrutal jungle fighting along a single-file track in tropical mountain terrain. Japanese reached Ioribaiwa Ridge, 32 miles from Port Moresby, by mid-September. Logistical exhaustion and the Guadalcanal drain forced withdrawal. AIF counter-attacked through October, driving Japanese back to Buna by November. First land defeat of Japanese forces in the war. Defining campaign of Australian national memory.

7 August 1942 – 9 February 1943

Guadalcanal Campaign [Bracket]

Forces1st USMC Div (Vandegrift), then 2nd USMC, US Army XIV Corps (Patch); USN South Pacific Force; RAN/RNZN ships; Cactus Air Force at Henderson Field; vs Japanese 17th Army + IJN Combined Fleet elements.
Intent (Allied)Seize the airfield under construction on Guadalcanal (Henderson Field) to deny Japan a base threatening US–Australia SLOCs; the first major US offensive of the war.
OutcomeSix-month grinding land/sea/air campaign of mutual attrition. Henderson held; Japanese evacuated remaining ~10,000 troops in Operation KE (1–7 Feb 1943). US: ~7,100 KIA across services. Japan: ~31,000 dead, ~1,000 aircraft, 2 BBs, 4 cruisers, 11 destroyers, 6 submarines. Land equivalent of Midway: Japan never again held strategic initiative in the South Pacific.

8–9 August 1942

Battle of Savo Island

ForcesTF 62.6 (Crutchley, RN, then Bode/Riefkohl): HMAS Canberra, USS Vincennes, Quincy, Astoria, Chicago, RN destroyers; vs Mikawa's 8th Fleet: 5 heavy cruisers, 2 light cruisers, 1 destroyer.
Intent (Allied)Screen the Guadalcanal landings against Japanese surface response approaching down "the Slot."
OutcomeWorst surface defeat in USN history. Canberra and three US heavy cruisers sunk in a 32-minute night action; ~1,077 Allied sailors killed against trivial Japanese losses. Mikawa failed to press on to the transports: controversial decision that saved the landing force. Tactical disaster but doctrinal catalyst: drove US night-fighting practice, radar tactics, and naval intelligence reform.

24–25 August 1942

Battle of the Eastern Solomons

ForcesTF 11/16/18 (Fletcher): USS Saratoga, Enterprise, Wasp; vs Striking Force (Nagumo) + Diversionary Group: Shōkaku, Zuikaku, light carrier Ryūjō.
Intent (Allied)Intercept the Japanese reinforcement convoy bound for Guadalcanal.
OutcomeRyūjō sunk; Enterprise heavily damaged; Japanese troop transports turned back. Tactical US victory; further attrition of irreplaceable Japanese carrier aircrew. Demonstrated that Coral Sea / Midway was not aberrant: carrier engagements would systematically favor the side with cryptographic and scouting advantage.

11–12 October 1942

Battle of Cape Esperance

ForcesTF 64 (Scott): USS San Francisco, Boise, Salt Lake City, Helena, 5 destroyers; vs Japanese cruiser–destroyer force (Gotō).
Intent (Allied)Intercept the Japanese "Tokyo Express" reinforcement run to Guadalcanal.
OutcomeFirst clear US night surface victory of the war. Japanese cruiser Furutaka and destroyer Fubuki sunk; Gotō killed. USS Duncan lost. Tactical victory complicated by friendly fire and a confused command picture, but the upward curve in US night doctrine was unmistakable.

25–27 October 1942

Battle of the Santa Cruz Islands

ForcesTF 16/17 (Kinkaid): USS Enterprise, Hornet; vs Combined Fleet carriers (Nagumo): Shōkaku, Zuikaku, Zuihō, Junyō.
Intent (Allied)Repel the Japanese carrier force supporting the major Guadalcanal land offensive against Henderson Field.
OutcomeTactical Japanese victory: Hornet lost; Enterprise damaged. Strategic Allied gain: Japanese carrier air groups effectively destroyed by cumulative losses across Midway, Eastern Solomons, and Santa Cruz. By year's end the IJN's carrier striking power was hollowed out for the duration.

12–15 November 1942

Naval Battle of Guadalcanal

ForcesTF 64 (Callaghan, then Lee): cruiser–destroyer force the first nights, then BBs Washington and South Dakota in the climactic action; vs Japanese Raiding Group (Abe), then Bombardment Group (Kondō) with BBs Hiei and Kirishima.
Intent (Allied)Prevent Japanese battleship bombardment of Henderson Field and disrupt the major reinforcement convoy.
OutcomeThree nights of brutal close-range action. Hiei (first IJN BB loss of the war) and Kirishima (sunk by Washington's 16-inch fire, first BB-vs-BB action since Jutland) lost; Callaghan and Scott killed in the first night. Japanese reinforcement convoy decimated by USMC air; only ~2,000 of 7,000 troops landed. Decisive engagement of the campaign: Japan never again seriously contested the seas around Guadalcanal.

30 November 1942

Battle of Tassafaronga

ForcesTF 67 (Wright): 5 cruisers, 6 destroyers; vs DesRon 2 (Tanaka): 8 destroyers.
Intent (Allied)Intercept the Japanese destroyer-run supply mission to Guadalcanal.
OutcomeTactical Japanese victory at the close of strategic Japanese defeat. Tanaka's destroyers landed no supplies but devastated Wright's force: USS Northampton sunk; Minneapolis, New Orleans, Pensacola badly damaged. Last Japanese tactical success in the waters around Guadalcanal. Tokyo decided on evacuation shortly thereafter.

16 November 1942 – 22 January 1943

Battle of Buna–Gona

ForcesUS 32nd Inf Div, AUS 7th Div (Vasey), AUS 18th Bde; vs Japanese Buna–Gona garrison + remnants of South Seas Detachment.
Intent (Allied)Reduce the Japanese coastal beachheads at Buna–Gona on the north coast of Papua following the Kokoda counter-pursuit; complete the destruction of South Seas Detachment.
OutcomeCostly, prolonged jungle siege of well-fortified positions. Gona fell 9 Dec, Buna 2 Jan, Sanananda 22 Jan. ~3,300 Allied KIA (~2,800 AIF); ~7,000 Japanese dead. With Guadalcanal, completed the strategic Japanese defeat of late 1942 and set the template for SWPA's island-by-island grind under MacArthur.

2–4 March 1943

Battle of the Bismarck Sea

Forces5th USAAF (Kenney) + RAAF: A-20s, B-17s, B-25s (some modified by Kenney's staff with forward-firing .50-cal batteries for masthead strafing: the "commerce destroyer" configuration); vs Japanese reinforcement convoy bound for Lae (8 transports, 8 destroyers).
Intent (Allied)Destroy the convoy ferrying 6,900 troops of Japanese 51st Division to Lae, New Guinea.
OutcomeAnnihilation. All 8 transports and 4 destroyers sunk; ~3,000 Japanese troops and sailors killed in the water; only ~850 troops reached Lae. Demonstrated the maturity of land-based air power against sea targets under air superiority. Japanese discontinued major surface reinforcement of New Guinea; thereafter relied on barges and submarines.

26 March 1943

Battle of the Komandorski Islands

ForcesTG 16.6 (McMorris): USS Salt Lake City, Richmond, 4 destroyers; vs Japanese force (Hosogaya): 2 heavy cruisers, 2 light cruisers, 4 destroyers covering an Attu/Kiska supply convoy.
Intent (Allied)Intercept the Japanese resupply mission to the Aleutian outposts.
OutcomeRare daylight gun duel with no air involvement on either side. McMorris's smaller force held off Hosogaya's heavier squadron for nearly four hours; Salt Lake City hit but survived. Hosogaya broke off in the false belief that US carrier air was inbound. The convoy turned back; thereafter Japanese could supply the Aleutians only by submarine.

18 April 1943

Operation Vengeance (Yamamoto Shootdown)

Forces339th FS / 347th FG (Mitchell mission commander; Lanphier and Barber on the killing flight), 16 P-38G Lightnings flying from Kukum Field, Guadalcanal; ~1,000-mile round trip, most at wave-top altitude under radio silence.
IntentIntercept and shoot down Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto's transport (Mitsubishi G4M "Betty"), carrying him on a forward-area morale inspection of Buin and Ballale; routing intelligence from FRUPac's break of JN-25, relayed via Nimitz with Roosevelt's personal authorization. Targeting a head of state's military equivalent on the basis of SIGINT: a precedent of lasting consequence.
OutcomeBoth Bettys shot down over the Bougainville jungle; Yamamoto killed in the first, his body recovered by Japanese ground party next day. One P-38 lost on return. Most consequential targeted killing of the Pacific war; removed the architect of Pearl Harbor and Midway, and the IJN's most capable operational mind at the moment when his replacement (Koga) lacked his political authority. Lanphier/Barber kill-credit dispute persists to this day; the AAF officially split the credit posthumously in 1985.

11–30 May 1943

Battle of Attu (Operation Landcrab)

ForcesUS 7th Inf Div (Brown, then Landrum); vs Japanese Aleutian garrison (Yamasaki) ~2,500.
Intent (Allied)Recapture the Aleutian outpost of Attu, US territory, and demonstrate that no Japanese-occupied US soil would remain.
OutcomeTwo and a half weeks of cold-weather mountain fighting. Final Japanese position eliminated 29 May in one of the largest banzai charges of the war: ~800 Japanese died in the assault, including wounded who suicided rather than be captured. ~3,000 US casualties (~550 KIA, ~1,200 wounded, ~1,200 cold-weather injuries). Highest US casualty ratio of any Pacific operation relative to forces engaged.

15 August 1943

Capture of Kiska (Operation Cottage)

ForcesUS 7th Div elements + Canadian 13th Inf Bde (~35,000); against a Japanese garrison that had quietly evacuated under cover of fog on 28 July.
Intent (Allied)Complete the recapture of the Aleutians after Attu.
OutcomeUnopposed landing: the Japanese had evacuated their ~5,000-man garrison by surface ships and submarines in the preceding weeks. ~32 Allied casualties from friendly fire, mines, and booby-traps. Aleutian Campaign closed; the diversion of disproportionate US and Canadian resources from the central Pacific ended.

30 June 1943 – 19 March 1944

Operation Cartwheel (Solomons / New Guinea)

ForcesSWPA (MacArthur) + SoPac (Halsey): US 6th Army, 3rd USMC Div, 25th and 43rd Inf Divs, NZ 3rd Div, AUS 7th and 9th Divs, RAAF, RNZAF; vs Japanese 17th and 18th Armies.
IntentNeutralize and isolate the major Japanese base at Rabaul through a series of coordinated amphibious operations up the Solomons chain and along the New Guinea north coast, the "leapfrog" / "island-hopping" doctrine, rather than reduce Rabaul by direct assault.
OutcomeNew Georgia (Jun–Aug 1943), Vella Lavella (Aug–Oct), Treasury / Bougainville (Nov onward), Cape Gloucester (Dec), Saidor (Jan 1944), Admiralties (Feb–Mar). Rabaul left to "wither on the vine" with ~100,000 isolated troops. Strategic doctrine validated; foundation for the parallel Central Pacific drive.

30 June – 25 August 1943

New Georgia / Munda Point Campaign (Operation Toenails)

ForcesXIV US Army Corps (Hester, then Griswold from 15 July): 43rd Inf Div, 37th Inf Div, 25th Inf Div elements; 1st Marine Raider Regiment; supporting USN Task Force 31 (Turner); vs Japanese New Georgia garrison under Sasaki: 229th Inf Regt (38th Div), 13th Inf Regt, naval base force, ~10,500 total around the Munda airfield. Naval companion battles handled by Task Forces 18, 36, 38 (Ainsworth, Merrill, Wilkinson): Kula Gulf (5–6 July), Kolombangara (12–13 July), Vella Gulf (6–7 August), Vella Lavella (6–7 October).
IntentSeize Munda Point airfield on New Georgia within Cartwheel's southern axis: the first step in neutralizing Bougainville and the road to Rabaul. Apply Guadalcanal's lessons at greater scale and against a fortified airfield objective.
OutcomeTwo months of jungle fighting that significantly exceeded planners' projections. Hester relieved 15 July for slow progress; Griswold reorganized and pressed the assault. Munda taken 5 August; remaining Japanese forces driven into the interior and progressively reduced through late August. ~1,100 US KIA, ~3,800 wounded, ~3,800 non-battle casualties: disease and combat-fatigue toll heavy. ~2,500 Japanese killed; the rest evacuated to Kolombangara and onward. Doctrinally significant: Sasaki's defense-in-depth at Munda (interlocking pillboxes, deep dugouts, reverse-slope positions) anticipated by some 18 months the tactics that would savage US forces at Peleliu, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa. The naval companion battles refined US night surface doctrine and finally closed the lessons of Tassafaronga. Often subsumed within the Cartwheel bracket; the doctrinal way-station warrants the separate entry.

20–23 November 1943

Battle of Tarawa (Operation Galvanic, Betio)

Forces2nd USMC Div (Smith); concurrent landing at Makin (US 27th Inf Div); vs Japanese SNLF garrison (Shibazaki) ~4,500 on Betio.
Intent (Allied)Seize Betio Island in the Tarawa Atoll (Gilberts) to open the Central Pacific drive toward the Marshalls.
Outcome76 hours of brutal close-quarter combat across 291 acres. ~3,000 USMC casualties (~1,000 KIA); virtually the entire Japanese garrison killed (17 prisoners). Wide doctrinal autopsy: tides over the reef miscalculated, LVT shortage costly, pre-bombardment naval gunfire and air inadequate. The lessons reshaped every subsequent amphibious operation in the Pacific.

1944

↑ Top

31 January – 3 February 1944

Battle of Kwajalein (Operation Flintlock, Marshalls)

ForcesUS 4th Marine Div, 7th Inf Div; supporting TF 58 carrier air; vs Japanese garrison ~8,000.
Intent (Allied)Apply Tarawa's lessons to the Marshalls; seize Kwajalein Atoll as fleet anchorage and air base.
OutcomeDecisive Allied victory at significantly reduced cost. Pre-landing bombardment doubled in weight; LVT-2/4 numbers tripled; UDT reconnaissance methodical. ~370 US KIA; virtually the entire Japanese garrison killed. Validated post-Tarawa doctrine; completed ~three months ahead of schedule, allowing Eniwetok to follow immediately rather than after a long pause.

17–18 February 1944

Operation Hailstone (Truk Raid)

ForcesTF 58 (Mitscher, under Spruance): 5 fleet carriers, 4 light carriers, 7 BBs, full screen; vs Japanese Combined Fleet base at Truk lagoon.
Intent (Allied)Neutralize "the Japanese Pearl Harbor," the major IJN fleet base at Truk, before Eniwetok and the Marianas operation.
OutcomeTwo days of strikes destroyed ~270 Japanese aircraft and sank ~50 vessels (~200,000 GRT) including 2 light cruisers, 4 destroyers, ~30 merchantmen. The Combined Fleet's main combatants had withdrawn to Palau a week earlier, but Truk's utility as an offensive base ended permanently. The fast carrier task force had become a strategic weapon capable of striking anywhere in the Pacific.

17–23 February 1944

Battle of Eniwetok (Operation Catchpole)

Forces22nd Marines, US 106th Inf Regt, V Amphibious Corps Reconnaissance Co; vs Japanese garrison ~3,500.
Intent (Allied)Complete the Marshalls campaign; secure a forward anchorage closer to the Marianas.
OutcomeAtoll secured in six days at ~340 US KIA. Confirmed the post-Tarawa doctrinal package: heavier naval gunfire support, integrated UDT, dedicated armored amphibian battalions. Eniwetok anchorage became the principal staging point for the Marianas operations.

22 April 1944

Hollandia–Aitape Landings (Operations Reckless / Persecution)

ForcesSWPA: US I Corps (Eichelberger): 24th, 41st Inf Divs at Hollandia; 32nd ID at Aitape; supported by TF 58 carrier strikes against Japanese air in the region.
IntentMacArthur's signature leapfrog: bypass the 50,000-strong Japanese 18th Army at Wewak by landing 200 miles to its rear at Hollandia, cutting its LOCs and isolating it.
OutcomeLight initial opposition; Hollandia airfields and harbor secured within four days. 18th Army cut off, subsequently destroyed piecemeal in the Driniumor River fighting through July–August. Among the most operationally elegant of MacArthur's operations; established the deep-leap template that would carry SWPA forces to the Philippines.

15 June – 9 July 1944

Battle of Saipan (Operation Forager phase)

ForcesV Amphibious Corps (Holland Smith): 2nd, 4th Marine Divs; 27th Inf Div; vs Japanese 43rd Div + Saipan garrison (Saitō, with Nagumo as senior naval officer ashore) ~31,000.
Intent (Allied)Seize Saipan as a B-29 base within range of Tokyo; force decisive carrier engagement; breach the inner defensive perimeter of Tōjō's "absolute defense line."
OutcomeBrutal three-week land battle. The northern cliffs witnessed mass military and civilian suicides as Japanese officers exhorted civilians not to surrender to the Americans. ~3,400 US KIA, ~13,000 wounded; ~24,000 Japanese KIA + ~5,000 civilians dead. Tōjō's cabinet fell. B-29 base construction began within weeks.

19–20 June 1944

Battle of the Philippine Sea ("Marianas Turkey Shoot")

ForcesTF 58 (Mitscher, under Spruance): 7 fleet + 8 light carriers, 956 aircraft; vs IJN 1st Mobile Fleet (Ozawa): 5 fleet + 4 light carriers, 473 aircraft.
Intent (Japanese)Decisive carrier engagement responding to the Saipan landings (A-Gō Sakusen). (Allied): Destroy the IJN's carrier force while protecting the amphibious lodgement.
OutcomeLopsided air victory. Three Japanese carriers sunk: Taihō and Shōkaku by US submarines (Albacore, Cavalla), Hiyō in the second day's strike. ~600 Japanese aircraft destroyed against ~123 US, the latter mostly from fuel exhaustion on the night recovery after Mitscher's "turn on the lights" order. Effectively annihilated Japanese carrier aviation as a strategic instrument. Spruance criticized for not pursuing, debated since, defended by most as the correct call given the amphibious shield mission.

21 July – 10 August 1944

Battle of Guam (Forager phase)

ForcesIII Amphibious Corps (Geiger): 3rd Marine Div, 1st Provisional Marine Bde, 77th Inf Div; vs Japanese garrison (Takashina, then Obata) ~18,500.
Intent (Allied)Recapture US territory lost in December 1941; expand the Marianas base complex.
OutcomeThree weeks of organized resistance; isolated Japanese holdouts continued for decades (Yokoi surrendered 1972; Onoda on Lubang in the Philippines, 1974). ~1,750 US KIA; ~18,000 Japanese KIA. Apra Harbor became a major Allied forward base.

24 July – 1 August 1944

Battle of Tinian (Forager phase)

Forces2nd, 4th Marine Divs (Schmidt); vs Japanese garrison ~8,000.
Intent (Allied)Complete the Marianas group; secure flat terrain suitable for a major B-29 base complex.
OutcomeOften called "the most perfectly executed amphibious operation of the war." Surprise landing on the unexpected northern beaches; nine days to secure; ~325 US KIA. Tinian's North Field became the largest airfield in the world by mid-1945: eventual launch point for the atomic missions.

15 September – 27 November 1944

Battle of Peleliu (Operation Stalemate II)

ForcesIII Amphibious Corps (Geiger): 1st Marine Div (Rupertus), 81st Inf Div; vs Japanese 14th Div garrison (Nakagawa) ~10,900.
Intent (Allied)Secure airfields on Peleliu to cover MacArthur's right flank on the Mindanao approach: operational logic that became questionable when the Mindanao operation was canceled in favor of direct assault on Leyte.
OutcomeRupertus's predicted "three days" became 73. The Japanese had adopted a new defense-in-depth doctrine using the Umurbrogol ridge cave complex, anticipating Iwo Jima and Okinawa. ~1,800 US KIA, ~8,000 wounded; 1st MarDiv took ~6,500 casualties of ~17,000: combat-ineffective for months. Strategic value disputed since; the doctrinal preview was useful, but the campaign itself was perhaps avoidable.

20 October 1944 – 8 May 1945

Philippines Campaign (Leyte → Luzon → Southern Islands) [Bracket]

ForcesSixth US Army (Krueger), Eighth US Army (Eichelberger); 7th Fleet (Kinkaid); supported by 3rd Fleet (Halsey); Filipino guerrilla networks; vs Japanese 14th Area Army (Yamashita) and naval/SNLF garrisons.
IntentLiberate the Philippines; sever Japan's southern resource LOCs to the East Indies; honor MacArthur's "I shall return."
OutcomeLeyte landings 20 Oct opened the campaign; Mindoro 15 Dec; Luzon landings at Lingayen Gulf 9 Jan 1945; Manila battle 3 Feb – 3 Mar 1945, the most destructive urban battle of the Pacific (~100,000 Filipino civilian dead, city devastated against Yamashita's stated intent: naval troops under Iwabuchi defied the planned withdrawal). ~62,000 US casualties; 14th Area Army's ~336,000 dead. Japanese SLOC to the East Indies severed.

23–26 October 1944

Battle of Leyte Gulf (Axis: Shō-Gō 1)

Forces7th Fleet (Kinkaid), 3rd Fleet (Halsey); RAN/RNZN escorts including HMAS Australia, Shropshire, Arunta; vs IJN Combined Fleet remnants (Toyoda): Center Force (Kurita), Southern Force (Nishimura/Shima), Northern Decoy Force (Ozawa).
Intent (Japanese)Destroy the US amphibious force off Leyte through a four-pronged convergence; bet what remained of the IJN on a final decisive blow. (Allied): Defend the beachhead; destroy the IJN.
OutcomeLargest naval engagement in history by tonnage and personnel. Four major actions: Sibuyan Sea (Musashi sunk); Surigao Strait (last battleship-vs-battleship action: Nishimura annihilated by Oldendorf's crossing-the-T); Cape Engaño (Ozawa's carriers destroyed, but the decoy succeeded in drawing Halsey north); Samar (Taffy 3's escort carriers and DDs/DEs, Sprague, Evans of Johnston, improbably repelled Kurita's center force in the war's most extraordinary defensive action). First widespread kamikaze (神風) attacks. IJN effectively ceased as a coherent strategic force.

17–18 December 1944

Halsey's Typhoon (Typhoon Cobra)

ForcesTF 38 (McCain, under Halsey): fast carrier task force conducting strikes in support of Philippines operations.
IntentContinue close air support of Philippines operations; refuel at sea between strikes.
OutcomeCaught by Typhoon Cobra in the Philippine Sea. Three destroyers (Hull, Monaghan, Spence) capsized and sank; ~790 sailors killed; ~146 aircraft destroyed or jettisoned; multiple ships damaged. Court of inquiry concluded Halsey had been at fault for failing to heed weather warnings, though "in extenuation thereof... should not be judged too harshly." Pacific Fleet meteorological practice substantially reformed.

June 1944 – August 1945

B-29 Strategic Bombing of Japan from the Marianas [Bracket]

ForcesXX Bomber Command (briefly, from China via Operation Matterhorn, June 1944 – Jan 1945, before being closed out as logistically untenable); XXI Bomber Command (Hansell, then LeMay from January 1945) from Saipan, Tinian, Guam: eventually 5 wings, ~1,000 B-29s. Supported by P-51D Mustangs flying escort from Iwo Jima (post-March 1945) and naval rescue from picket submarines and Dumbo air-sea-rescue PBYs.
IntentBurn out Japanese war-making capacity through sustained heavy-bomber operations against the home islands; initial doctrine of high-altitude daylight precision bombing (abandoned after poor results from the jet stream and target obscuration), then low-altitude night incendiary attacks under LeMay; force a decision short of the projected invasion.
OutcomeMost destructive air campaign in history measured by area burned and population displaced. ~67 Japanese cities firebombed; ~330,000 Japanese killed across the campaign; ~2.5M housing units destroyed; ~9M displaced. Combined with the naval blockade and submarine campaign, brought Japanese industrial production to near-collapse by July 1945. Operationally costly: ~400 B-29s lost (mostly to weather, mechanical failure, mining-mission losses, night fighter/flak combinations); ~3,000 crew dead. Inaugurated city-burning as the closing logic of the war and seeded postwar US air-power doctrine.

1945

↑ Top

19 February – 26 March 1945

Battle of Iwo Jima (Operation Detachment)

ForcesV Amphibious Corps (Schmidt): 3rd, 4th, 5th Marine Divs; vs Japanese 109th Div (Kuribayashi) ~21,000.
IntentSeize Iwo Jima as fighter-escort base for B-29s en route to Japan and as emergency landing field for damaged bombers returning.
Outcome36 days of the hardest fighting in USMC history. Kuribayashi's defense-in-depth through 11 miles of volcanic tunnels (no banzai charges, no beach defense) produced ~26,000 US casualties (~6,800 KIA); ~21,000 Japanese KIA (216 prisoners). Iconic Suribachi flag-raising 23 Feb (Rosenthal). The only Pacific battle in which US casualties exceeded Japanese. ~2,400 B-29 emergency landings recorded by war's end; strategic value modest relative to cost.

9–10 March 1945

Operation Meetinghouse (Tokyo Firebombing)

ForcesXXI Bomber Command (LeMay): 279 B-29s from Guam, Tinian, Saipan; stripped of defensive armament, loaded with M-69 incendiary clusters; low-altitude (5,000–9,000 ft) night attack.
IntentInflict maximum damage on the cottage-industry war workshops dispersed through Tokyo residential districts after high-altitude precision daylight bombing failed to produce results; force decision through population pressure.
Outcome~16 square miles of Tokyo burned out; ~100,000 civilians killed in the single deadliest air attack of the war: a greater immediate death toll than either atomic strike; ~one million made homeless. Inaugurated the Marianas-based firebombing campaign that destroyed most major Japanese cities by August. Strategic effect debated: Japanese surrender attributable to firebombing + naval blockade + atomic strikes + Soviet entry, not any single factor.

27 March – 14 August 1945

Operation Starvation (Aerial Mining of Japanese Home Waters)

Forces313th BW, XXI Bomber Command (Davies); B-29s carrying Mark 25, 26, and 36 acoustic, magnetic, and pressure mines from Tinian.
IntentClose Japan's home waters to shipping by aerially mining the Shimonoseki Strait (the choke point between Honshū and Kyūshū through which most coastal traffic moved) and the approaches to Kobe, Osaka, Tokyo Bay, and the Inland Sea.
Outcome~12,000 mines laid in 4½ months. ~670 ships sunk or damaged (~1.25M GRT): more tonnage destroyed per sortie than any other USAAF operation of the war. Shimonoseki effectively closed; Kobe and Osaka approaches blocked; surviving traffic forced into the Sea of Japan where USN submarines waited. Combined with the submarine campaign, Japanese tanker imports from the Asian mainland collapsed in spring 1945. LeMay's reluctant but eventual endorsement; in postwar strategic-bombing analysis, the most cost-effective B-29 operation of the war.

1 April – 22 June 1945

Battle of Okinawa (Operation Iceberg; Axis: Ten-Gō / Kikusui)

ForcesTenth US Army (Buckner): III Amphibious Corps (1st, 6th MarDivs), XXIV Corps (7th, 27th, 77th, 96th Inf Divs); naval support TF 58 + British Pacific Fleet TF 57; vs Japanese 32nd Army (Ushijima) ~100,000 + Okinawan conscripts.
IntentSeize Okinawa as the staging base for the projected invasion of Kyushu (Operation Olympic); demonstrate to Tokyo that the home islands themselves could be assaulted.
Outcome82 days of attritional combat: Shuri Line, Sugar Loaf Hill, Hacksaw Ridge, the cave-and-tunnel reduction in the south. ~14,000 US KIA + ~50,000 wounded/non-battle; ~110,000 Japanese KIA; ~100,000+ Okinawan civilian dead. Largest kamikaze effort of the war (the Kikusui, "floating chrysanthemums," series of ten mass attacks): ~1,900 sorties damaged or sank ~36 US ships. Buckner killed 18 June by Japanese artillery. The cost informed the atomic decision regarding Olympic.

7 April 1945

Sinking of Yamato (Operation Ten-Ichi-Gō)

ForcesTF 58 (Mitscher): aircraft from Hornet, Bennington, Belleau Wood, San Jacinto, Essex, Bunker Hill, Hancock, Bataan, Wasp, Yorktown, Langley: 386 aircraft in two strikes; vs IJN Surface Special Attack Force (Itō Seiichi): Yamato, light cruiser Yahagi, 8 destroyers.
Intent (Japanese)One-way surface sortie to beach Yamato at Okinawa as a stationary battery and as a decoy diverting US attention from kikusui aerial kamikaze attacks: fuel for the return leg never loaded. "The last sortie of the Imperial Japanese Navy."
OutcomeTF 58 located the force southwest of Kyūshū at 1232. Two hours of air attack: ~10 torpedo hits and ~7 bomb hits on Yamato; magazine explosion at 1423; she capsized and sank within minutes. ~3,055 of her ~3,300 crew killed including Itō. Yahagi and four destroyers sunk. Ten US aircraft lost. The largest battleship ever built eliminated by carrier air alone: final empirical demonstration that the battleship era ended in the Pacific war it had been designed to dominate.

March – August 1945

British Pacific Fleet (Task Force 57) Operations

ForcesBPF (Rawlings, under Spruance/Halsey): 4 fleet carriers (Indomitable, Victorious, Illustrious, Indefatigable), 2 BBs (King George V, Howe), cruisers and DDs; Australian, NZ, Canadian elements integrated.
IntentDemonstrate British re-entry into the Pacific war following the European victory; contribute carrier strikes against the Sakishima Gunto airfields (between Formosa and Okinawa) used by Japanese aircraft staging to Okinawa; later contribute to strikes against Japan proper.
OutcomeSustained strikes through Iceberg suppressed Sakishima staging. BPF later joined TF 38 strikes on Honshū and Hokkaidō (July–August). British carriers' armored flight decks proved valuable absorbing kamikaze hits with limited operational loss: a doctrinal vindication. Operationally significant; politically vital to Britain's seat at the Pacific peace.

June 1945 – 15 August 1945

Operation Downfall: Olympic / Coronet (Planned Invasion of Japan)

Forces (Planned)Sixth US Army (Krueger): Olympic landing force, 14 divisions across X, XI, and V Amphibious Corps; Eighth and First US Armies for Coronet, ~25 divisions; UK Commonwealth Corps for Coronet (1 UK, 1 Cdn, 1 AUS Div proposed); supporting BPF and TF 38/58; ~767,000 troops in the Olympic initial assault, scaling to ~1.5M for the combined operations. Vs projected Japanese defense under Ketsu-Gō: 16th Area Army (Yokoyama) on Kyushu with ~600,000 trained troops; ~10,000 kamikaze aircraft conserved specifically for invasion-fleet attack (the 1944–45 air losses, see Marianas Turkey Shoot and Okinawa, having been deliberately reckoned acceptable to husband this reserve); ~100 midget submarines (kōryū, kairyū), kaiten manned torpedoes, shinyō suicide motor boats; Volunteer Fighting Corps (Kokumin Giyū Sentōtai) conscripted civilian formations including women and adolescents.
IntentForce unconditional Japanese surrender by invasion of the home islands. Olympic (X-Day target 1 November 1945): landings on southern Kyushu (Miyazaki, Ariake Bay, Kushikino); seize southern Kyushu as a forward air base and forcing point against the Kantō plain. Coronet (Y-Day target 1 March 1946): main landings on Honshū at Sagami Bay and Kujūkuri Beach; envelop and seize Tokyo.
OutcomeNever executed. Casualty estimates that fed into the atomic decision varied widely: Joint War Plans Committee June 1945 estimate ~193,000 US casualties (Olympic + Coronet); MacArthur's lower projection; Marshall's working figure ~250,000; Hoover's June estimate cited to Truman of ~500,000–1,000,000 US dead; postwar revisions in the 1990s found Japanese defensive preparations on Kyushu by August 1945 substantially exceeded contemporaneous Allied intelligence: Ultra picked up 14 IJA divisions on Kyushu by August against the planners' assumption of 6, and the kamikaze reserve was larger than projected. The Purple Heart medals struck in anticipation of Olympic/Coronet (~500,000) supplied US military casualties through the next 70 years of conflict, including Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, and were not fully exhausted until well into the 21st century. The atomic bombings, Soviet entry into the war on 9 August (Manchurian Strategic Offensive: Маньчжурская стратегическая наступательная операция; see Notes), and surrender on 15 August foreclosed the operation; the planning context remains essential to understanding the strategic calculus of summer 1945.

August 1942 – August 1945

Manhattan Engineer District [Program Bracket]

ForcesManhattan Engineer District (USACE) under Maj Gen Leslie Groves; scientific direction by J. Robert Oppenheimer at Los Alamos (Y-Site); production at Oak Ridge (K-25 gaseous diffusion, Y-12 electromagnetic separation for U-235) and Hanford (B, D, F reactors and T Plant for plutonium); the Metallurgical Laboratory at Chicago (Compton, Fermi) where CP-1 achieved the first self-sustaining chain reaction on 2 December 1942; British contribution via the Tube Alloys project (Chadwick, Peierls, Frisch, Cockcroft), formally integrated under the Quebec Agreement (August 1943, see L1 Quadrant entry) and modified by the Hyde Park Aide-Mémoire (September 1944); Combined Policy Committee (Stimson, Bush, Conant; Anderson, Dill; Howe) for US–UK–Canadian coordination. Origins traced to the Einstein–Szilárd letter to FDR (October 1939), the resulting Briggs Uranium Committee (1939–41), and decisively to the British MAUD Committee report (July 1941) that established weapon feasibility on a clear timeline: the document that converted the US program from research to engineering.
IntentDevelop fission weapons before Germany could; later, secure operational weapons in time to compel Japanese surrender without the projected invasion of the home islands (see Olympic / Coronet entry below). Pursued two parallel weapon paths: gun-type uranium (Little Boy, requiring no proof test given the design's analytic simplicity) and implosion-type plutonium (Fat Man, whose hydrodynamic complexity required the Trinity demonstration).
Outcome~$2B program cost (1945 dollars); ~130,000 employed at peak across 30+ sites; ~600,000 cleared personnel over the program's span. Chronology: Briggs Committee (1939) → S-1 Committee (June 1942) → Manhattan Engineer District established 13 August 1942 → CP-1 critical 2 December 1942 → Quebec Agreement August 1943 → Los Alamos populated 1943–44 → Trinity at Alamogordo, NM, 16 July 1945 (plutonium implosion "gadget," ~21 kt yield) → operational deployment via 509th Composite Group from Tinian North Field, August 1945. Soviet penetration: the program was extensively read by Soviet intelligence through Klaus Fuchs (British Tube Alloys delegation at Los Alamos), Theodore Hall (Los Alamos), David Greenglass (Los Alamos machinist), and the Ovakimyan / Kheifets / Yatskov / Feklisov handler network, which is why Stalin's reaction to Truman's deliberately oblique Potsdam mention of a "new weapon" (24 July 1945) struck Truman as oddly indifferent; Сталин уже знал: he had been briefed in greater detail than Truman had divulged. The first Soviet test (RDS-1, "First Lightning," 29 August 1949) closely paralleled the Fat Man design. The wider postwar consequences (restructured international politics, nuclear deterrence, the arms race) beyond the scope of these lists; the operational deployment is the entry that follows.

6 and 9 August 1945

Atomic Bombings (Hiroshima, Nagasaki)

Forces509th Composite Group (Tibbets), 393rd Bomb Squadron: B-29s Enola Gay (Tibbets, Hiroshima) and Bockscar (Sweeney, Nagasaki); supported by reconnaissance, escort, and instrumentation aircraft from Tinian's North Field.
IntentCompel Japanese acceptance of the Potsdam terms without the projected invasion of the home islands (estimated US casualties for Olympic + Coronet running from ~250,000 to over 1,000,000 depending on the assumptions used); demonstrate the new weapon's strategic effect.
OutcomeHiroshima: ~70,000 killed immediately, ~140,000 dead by year's end. Nagasaki: ~40,000 killed immediately, ~70,000 dead by year's end. Combined with Soviet entry on 9 August, precipitated the Imperial Conference's acceptance of Potsdam (with retention of the Emperor) on 14 August. Closed WWII; opened the nuclear age.

2 September 1945

Japanese Surrender (USS Missouri, Tokyo Bay)

ForcesAllied delegation: MacArthur (SCAP), Nimitz (USN); UK, USSR, China, Australia, Canada, France, Netherlands, NZ signatories; Japanese delegation: Shigemitsu (government), Umezu (military).
IntentFormal acceptance of unconditional surrender per the Potsdam Declaration; establishment of Allied occupation authority.
OutcomeInstrument of Surrender signed aboard Missouri under the colors that had flown at Pearl Harbor and Perry's flag of 1853. Allied military occupation of Japan began. End of WWII.

Japanese Strategic Decision-Making and the Road to War

StructureNo civilian Prime Minister directed the war effort with unitary authority: the Imperial Japanese Army (IJA) under the Army Ministry and the Imperial Japanese Navy (IJN) under the Navy Ministry operated as parallel and frequently antagonistic strategic actors, coordinating through the Liaison Conference (Daihon'ei Seifu Renraku Kaigi, from November 1937) and the Imperial Conference (御前会議, gozen kaigi, with the Emperor presiding). The two services trained, equipped, and procured separately: the IJN built its own merchant marine, ran its own air arm, fielded its own ground forces (Rikusentai, Special Naval Landing Forces), and pursued strategic geometries that did not match: the IJA fixated on continental Asia and the Northern Strategy (Hokushin-ron, advance against the USSR), the IJN on the Pacific and the Southern Strategy (Nanshin-ron, advance toward the Southern Resource Area).
DoctrineThe Northern–Southern argument was institutionally settled at Khalkhin Gol / Nomonhan jiken (Халхин-Гол, May–September 1939), where Zhukov's 1st Army Group inflicted ~45,000 casualties on the Kwantung Army's 6. Gun. Hokushin-ron lost its empirical case, and the IJN's southward orientation became default by elimination: без Халхин-Гола не было бы Пёрл-Харбора overstates the case but not by much. The China quagmire after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident (Rokōkyō jiken, 7 July 1937) bound ~800,000–1.5M Japanese troops on the mainland by 1940 without prospect of decisive victory. The Tripartite Pact (27 September 1940) committed Japan to the Axis without German consultation on Pacific strategy; the US scrap-metal embargo (September 1940) and the freezing of Japanese assets following the Indochina occupation (July 1941) cut petroleum imports against Japan's two-year reserve. Yamamoto's Pearl Harbor concept, a strategic gambit against an enemy whose long-term industrial superiority he understood better than most of his colleagues, emerged through 1941 as the alternative to either war by stages or capitulation on the China question. Hirohito's role remained constitutionally constrained but decisively present at the threshold moments: the 6 September 1941 Imperial Conference that accepted preparations for war, and the surrender seidan of August 1945.
Operational ConsequencesDecision-making throughout the war retained the inter-service character. Operations from Midway (June 1942) onward were repeatedly compromised by the IJN's reluctance to share intelligence with the IJA, by IJA refusal to release shipping for fleet logistics, and by parallel production of incompatible aircraft types: the Ki-43 and the A6M built to different specifications for the same nominal mission. The Supreme War Council (Saikō Sensō Shidō Kaigi, the "Big Six") that took the surrender decision in August 1945 was the institutional reckoning of this structural fragmentation: six men, three IJA and three IJN, deadlocked 3–3 on acceptance of the Potsdam Declaration after Hiroshima and the Soviet declaration of war, with the Emperor's seidan ("sacred decision") of 9–10 August breaking the tie. The 14–15 August attempted coup (Kyūjō jiken) by IJA junior officers to seize the surrender recording before broadcast was the structural pattern's last operational expression. Individual entries throughout this list note specific decisions where they occur; this synthesizing note frames the strategic pattern.
