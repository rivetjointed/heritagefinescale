Context

The Battle of the Atlantic was Germany's sustained campaign to sever the sea lanes connecting Britain and North America: the lifeline on which Allied strategy in every other theater ultimately depended. At its broadest it ran from the first day of the war to V-E Day, a campaign of nearly six years with no single decisive engagement and no fixed front.

The chart captures four inflection points annotated on the visualization. The First Happy Time (June 1940) followed the fall of France; Dönitz's boats now operated from Biscay ports and hunted largely unsupported convoys with devastating effect. Paukenschlag (Operation Drumbeat, January 1942), soon compounded by the 1 February blackout of the Shark Enigma key, plunged Allied losses to their worst sustained level; the American eastern seaboard offered U-boats what amounted to a second happy time in coastal waters not yet organized into convoys. Shark was broken by Bletchley Park in December 1942, partially restoring convoy routing intelligence. Schwarzer Mai (Black May, 1943) marked the tipping point: forty-one U-boats sunk in a single month, against losses that could no longer be sustained; Dönitz withdrew the bulk of his force from the North Atlantic on 24 May.

The lower ribbon makes the strategic arithmetic visible: Allied shipbuilding, dominated from 1942 by American Liberty and Victory ship production, outpaced U-boat sinkings by an accelerating margin from mid-1942. By 1944, new construction exceeded losses by a factor that rendered the submarine campaign strategically irreversible.
