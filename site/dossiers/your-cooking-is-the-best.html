Pvt. Eugene C. Brent, 39621994 · Company G, 41st Armored Infantry, 2nd Armored Division

Debrief
He wrote that he had nothing to leave. His legacy refutes that.

Helena

Eugene Carpenter Brent was born June 1909 in Chicago, Illinois and came to Montana with his parents as a boy. He went through St. Helena grade school and Mt. St. Charles (now renamed Carroll College) and settled into a steady job in Helena with the United States Treasury's disbursing office in the Federal Building downtown. There he rose to assistant federal disbursing officer by the time he was drafted. He married Sylvia Smith of Miles City in February 1937. Three sons soon followed: Eugene Jr. in 1938, David in 1941, and Howard in June 1944. The family lived on Sixth Avenue, only a block away from his parents.

The family had already lost a brother 26 years earlier in World War I. His older brother, Ray Brent, was killed in France on 27 May 1918. He was a machine gunner with the 1st Division and was the first Montanan killed in action in that global conflict. The same obituary mentions another family hallmark: descent, through their mother Harriet Van Buren, from the 8th President of the United States, Martin Van Buren.

The events of December 7, 1941 reached every corner of America. Eugene's experience survives in one account, set down eighty-four years later by his eldest son:

"He tried to volunteer; the day after Pearl Harbor he was at the recruitment center in line. Aunt Mabel heard about it and called my mother. They both went down there and pulled him out of line."

Recalled by his eldest son, who was three years old in December 1941, from family oral history.

He tried to go on the first day but his sister and his wife stopped him. Twenty-five months later, the draft came for him anyway.

39621994

Army serial numbers were coded, not sequential. The leading 3 marks a Selective Service inductee: a drafted man, not a volunteer. The second digit 9 is the Ninth Service Command: Montana and the western states. The Montana Adjutant General's card fills in the rest: means of entry, induction; date of entry, 17 January 1944; married, three dependents. The man who tried to volunteer on 8 December 1941 was pulled into the maelstrom 2 years later, at age 34.

From reception at Fort Douglas the pipeline ran to Camp Croft, South Carolina, an Infantry Replacement Training Center outside Spartanburg. It was created to produce individual riflemen, seventeen weeks at a time, who would be dropped in where the needs called the most. He kept a copy of the post newspaper from that June: eleven thousand infantrymen marching past the reviewing stand in a downpour for Infantry Day. Somewhere in those wet ranks is a 34-year-old father of three from Montana.

By 19 July he was at Fort Meade, Maryland, in final staging, writing home about paperwork: a war-bond allotment of $18.75 a month to start the first of September, and instructions for Sylvia to send in Howard's birth certificate, whose allotment could not be processed without it. By his son David's memory, Eugene never had the chance to meet his youngest son. The same letter contains the only sentence where he takes stock of himself:

"We were told to make a will since we will be over seas by the first of Sept. but seeing that I have nothing to leave in a will I didn't make one."

Pvt. E. C. Brent to Sylvia Brent, Fort Meade, Md., 19 July 1944

He shipped overseas that summer. His eldest son's account adds another detail from the family history: He got the chance to spend about two weeks in Paris for a staging pause in the newly liberated city, and then forward, by truck, toward the German border.

Less than 2 Weeks

His company is fixed by his own hand. The return address he wrote on an envelope home reads Co. G – 41st Arm'd. Inf. Regt., A.P.O. 252 — Company G, 41st Armored Infantry Regiment, one of the three core combat formations of "Hell On Wheels", the 2nd Armored Division. After fighting through North Africa, the division had landed in Normandy three days after D-Day and had been fighting their way through Europe since. By the time it paused on the Belgian–German border in September, its depleted rifle companies were being refilled with replacements like Eugene, one man at a time.

The Army recorded the day he became a combat infantryman. General Order No. 17, Headquarters 41st Armored Infantry, awards the Combat Infantryman Badge "for exemplary conduct against the enemy in France since date as indicated" — and under Company G, date heading 24 Sept/44, stands Pvt Eugene O. Brent, 39621994. The serial number is exact; the middle initial is a clerk's error, one keystroke off, on his first combat decoration.

On 2 October, XIX Corps opened its assault on the Westwall north of Aachen. The 2nd Armored Division was pushed through the breach, into a belt of concrete built precisely to stop it. Dragon's teeth and pillboxes made this dismounted work: armored infantry out of their half-tracks, reducing fortifications one at a time, under artillery firing from beyond anyone's sight. The Chaplain who buried him described the day without ornament, in a letter to Sylvia the following August: the regiment "had just broken through the Siegfried Line at Ubach, Germany, and was making an attack on fortified positions there. He was killed instantly by the explosion of an enemy artillery shell."

The date on his badge to that unfortunate day was less than two weeks.

24 October 1944

Eighteen days after his death, the news reached Montana at telegram speed — and the Army's paperwork, running at its own speed, produced two documents on the same date. In Washington, the Adjutant General's office wired Helena. In Germany, Headquarters 41st Armored Infantry signed General Order No. 17, the badge order above, by order of Colonel Hinds. His Combat Infantryman Badge and his death notification carry the same date. Neither office knew what the other was doing, both on time by their own clocks.

The same order carries a second casualty of that worn ribbon: it was signed by order of Colonel WINDS — the H of Hinds struck without its crossbar, the identical slip that made Eugene an "O." two lines from his own name. Colonel Sidney R. Hinds commanded the regiment; the machine simply failed him too.

The wire went to his father, not his wife: Eugene had named Joseph as the person to be notified in an emergency, a detail the War Department explained to Sylvia in its letter of 29 October. The town read about their fallen son the next morning — page one of the Independent Record, 25 October, under "Father of Three Boys Was With 41st Armored Infantry." His parents had received letters postmarked England, France, Belgium. His last letter said he was going to Holland.

The telegram had promised "LETTER FOLLOWS," and the letters followed for years. The Adjutant General's on 29 October, with its apology that casualty reports "do not always reveal details of the heroic actions which led to their deaths." Ten months on, answering an inquiry from Sylvia who would not let it rest, came the letter that told her what actually happened. The regimental chaplain, William F. Overhulser, was able to name the place: the United States Military Cemetery at Margraten, Holland, three miles east of Maastricht. "His grave number is 83, row 4, plot 'A'."

The Ground

His first resting place is one of the most beautiful on earth, and it is kept that way on purpose. The Netherlands American Cemetery is sixty-five acres of lawn, lindens, and roses that lies three miles east of Maastricht. It is administered by the American Battle Monuments Commission, whose gardeners hold it to a standard these photographs only hint at. More than eight thousand American war dead lie beneath its crosses and Stars of David, arranged in long curved rows; another 1,722 names, Eugene's regiment among them, are cut into the Walls of the Missing.

The Dutch have never let these cherished men be forgotten. Under a grave-adoption program begun in 1945, stretching unbroken into this century, every grave and every name on the wall is adopted by a local family that visits it, lays flowers, and hands the duty down to its children. Eugene's grave was tended by strangers in Limburg long before his own family rediscovered it. That the cemetery kept its records so faithfully is the only reason the next part of this story is possible. (See the cemetery's history [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_American_Cemetery] for the fuller account.)

It was not always this garden. Margraten was the First Army's cemetery, cut into Dutch Limburg farmland in the autumn of 1944. It was close enough to the front line that the men who dug it could hear the battles raging. A wartime photograph shows what plot A looked like when Eugene lay in it; simple mounded earth and wooden crosses to the horizon.

The cemetery the pilgrim visits today is not the one the chaplain described. Its plots were reorganized in the years after its first ground-break and the wartime plot letters stopped meaning anything on the ground. The family packet from the cemetery itself held what was needed to find him regardless: a plan of the temporary cemetery, its plots lettered and each holding twelve rows of twenty-two graves, with the old grid drawn straight onto a present-day aerial. The wartime ground etched against the modern.

Find plot A on the plan, count in to row 4, and carry that square onto the overlay, centering on the front of the old cemetery: his grave falls outside today's burial fields entirely. It lies under the line of London plane trees just outside the brick boundary wall encircling the graveyard, in the mowed grass where no stone stands.

He came home in 1949. The body lay in state at the Chapel of Roses, rosary at the cathedral on Sunday with requiem mass following on Monday morning. He was buried with military honors from the American Legion. In March 1949 his father Joseph, by then living in Portland, Oregon, where much of the family had resettled, filed out the application for a government headstone. It was to be shipped to Eugene's brother Clement in Montana, who would have it properly set at Resurrection Cemetery in Helena. It stands today.

The Bench

In January 2020, a build honoring Eugene began on the Heritage Fine Scale bench. Though not an actual scene recalled by the record (the record was still years from being assembled), it was an imagined one: a battery camp behind the line, a six-by-six with a towed howitzer, ammunition crates and duffels, sandbagged revetments, a crew at rest.

The methods were the same ones this dossier runs on. The camp's road sign was hand-built from plastic structural stock and clay and carries distances plotted on modern mapping from the regiment's proximate position back to real cities, then printed at scale and set on the post. This is the Margraten overlay's instinct, pointed the other direction. The groundwork began the way groundwork always does — hundreds of cobbles rolled from clay, one stone at a time, graded by size across the cutting mat.

The build was interrupted and then lost in a move a year later. The camp will be rebuilt, however, now resting on the research it did not have the first time. This dossier is the first stage of that second build.

Debrief

The cold, documented version of Eugene Brent is the one calculated through arithmetic: a man killed in Germany, one of four hundred thousand American dead, an interchangeable replacement in a division whose famous battles stand in their own right. Drafted, shipped, killed while the system itself filed him under a wrong middle initial and a birthday he never wrote.

This record is accurate but not just. He was a 34-year-old Treasury man with three sons who tried to volunteer the day after Pearl Harbor, was pulled from the line by his wife and sister, and was taken anyway two years later by a manpower machine reaching into its last age brackets. It made him a rifleman in seventeen weeks, moved him through Paris in a replacement stream, and spent him in less than two weeks. A whirlwind so fast that his badge for entering combat and the telegram reporting his death were signed on the same October day, on two continents, by offices that never compared notes.

His own legacy outstrips this arithmetic: The man who wrote in July that he had "nothing to leave in a will" turned out to be wrong by the length and width of these pages. Sylvia, widowed at thirty, demanded the truth until she got it in writing. A registered nurse, she outlived Eugene by twenty-one years and delivered his grandson before she herself went home to rest in Miles City. He was survived by his sons and grandchildren who still lay flowers on his grave. In a history recorded by the county card file, the newspaper morgue, and a box of envelopes with pencil postscripts, his life refuses to stay filed. The Army's paperwork recorded him twice on the day it mattered most and got details wrong both times. The family's dedication got it right and is still correcting the Army's, eighty years on.

The camp on the bench will be rebuilt, its howitzer re-crewed, its road sign re-lettered with distances measured from a village the morning reports will name in time. The build inherits everything above with one standing order, in Eugene's own hand: Your cooking is the best.

Open Questions

A dossier earns trust by naming what it does not know. As of July 2026:

  • The village. The chaplain fixes the regiment at Ubach; the exact location of Company G on 6 October awaits the 41st AIR morning reports, NARA RG 407 — not yet requested.
  • The letters. Not yet read in full. Whether he was granted a leave, and how the gaps between postmarks fall, would show there.
  • The Margraten re-plan. The temporary-cemetery plan and an annotated overlay are now in hand — the family's Margraten packet — and place the first grave against the modern ground. The reorganization dates to 1944 or 1945; the exact year is left open at that range.
  • The photograph. The four-soldier portrait: date, place, and the other three men unidentified.

Recently settled: his date of birth is 24 June 1909 (his own draft card and the Montana service card; the stone's June 26 is his father's error). Induction was 17 January 1944 (Montana AGO card). Burial is Resurrection Cemetery, Helena, confirmed at the grave. Sylvia Smith Brent was born 27 January 1914 and died 17 May 1965, buried in her native Miles City. By David's memory, Eugene never met Howard.
