Highland Park

Joseph Vincent Ducceschi was born 4 March 1913 in Clover, in the coal country of western Pennsylvania. His father, Stephen, was a Tuscan immigrant and, by his daughter’s account, a proud one. His mother was Maria. At home the boy was Giuseppe. To everyone else, for the rest of his life, he was Joe.

His sister Alma was two years older and had the measure of her brothers early.

“My older brother was a devil, and my kid brother was a more calm boy.”

Alma Sbrilli, 82, Home News, 6 June 1994

They took each other’s side in everything, she said. Joe was the better student, and although he was the younger they ended up in the same grade; Alma credited him with getting her through the eighth. When the graduation test came she wanted the seat next to his, she admitted at eighty-two, “so I could do some cheating.”

When Joe was fourteen the coal work gave out and the Ducceschis went east to Highland Park, New Jersey, across the Raritan from New Brunswick. There was not much work in New Jersey either; Stephen took what there was, which was washing dishes. Times were hard enough that none of the Ducceschi children finished high school. Alma had a job at Bond Clothes in New Brunswick, a maker of men’s suits and one of the city’s large employers, and got her kid brother on there beside her. The two of them stayed close.

The Sbrillis remembered him coming home from Fort Benning in the uniform. “He loved that uniform, oh my God,” Alma said, with the snapshots in front of her: a handsome, strapping Joe on the family porch in Highland Park, and Joe behind a gun mounted in a jeep at Benning. On the porch he stands with Alma and her two boys, Fred Jr. and the baby, Robert. Fred Jr., nine that summer of 1944, remembered him as the favorite uncle, the one who came home on leave, collected the boy and every friend he had, and marched them all to Donaldson Park on the river for an Army-style cookout of hot dogs and baked potatoes. Fred counted it one of the best days of his childhood.

“He was just a nice, gentle guy.”

Fred Sbrilli Jr., Home News, 6 June 1994

32068439

Army serial numbers were coded, not counted. The leading 3 marks a Selective Service draftee. The 2 places his induction in the Second Corps Area, which took in New York, New Jersey and Delaware. The rest of the number sits low in a block that opened with the first peacetime draft in the autumn of 1940, which points to an early call. The family puts it in 1941, when he was twenty-eight. By then he and Alma had moved on from Bond Clothes to a sheepskin factory in town, and that is where he was working when he went in. “He wanted to get it over with,” Fred Sbrilli Sr. said. “Them days, they took everybody.” Alma remembered no reservations at all. The patriotism came with the Pennsylvania upbringing, and Joe held that every man going over went “with one thought in mind,” which was Hitler.

The number outlived every version of the name. It was issued once, at induction, and after that it followed him: onto the payroll and the morning report, the casualty list, the grave registration, and at last the burial register at Colleville-sur-Mer. A name could be shortened, misheard, or misspelled by a clerk who had never seen it before, and Ducceschi was all three at one time or another. The number could not. It is the single unbroken thread from a draft board in New Jersey to a white marble cross above the Channel, and it is the thread this page followed.

Alma’s snapshot fixes a place. The 4th Division was reactivated at Fort Benning on 1 June 1940 with the 8th, 22nd and 29th Infantry Regiments, trained there as a motorized division, and moved to Camp Gordon, near Augusta, in December 1941; the 12th Infantry had replaced the 29th that autumn. If Joe was with the division when the shutter clicked at Benning, and nothing in the record argues otherwise, he was a soldier before the country was at war.

The Experiment

He was in uniform for about three years before he saw a shot fired. The reason has nothing to do with him and everything to do with Army bureaucracy. In August 1940, ten weeks after the 4th Division came back onto the rolls, the Army picked it to test an idea: a division that rode instead of marched, the American answer to what the Germans had just done to Poland and France. The title became official on 11 July 1941, the 4th Motorized Division. For the next three years the division’s job was to prove a theory, and Joe’s job was to be in it.

The proving began in Louisiana. The division moved to Dry Prong on 1 August 1941 and went into the Louisiana Maneuvers of August and September, the largest exercises the United States Army had ever run: nineteen divisions and something near four hundred thousand men, fighting a mock war across thirty-four hundred square miles between the Sabine and the Calcasieu. For the maneuvers the 4th Motorized was put into I Armored Corps beside the 2nd Armored Division. It was back at Benning on 27 August, moved to Fort Jackson on 30 October for the First Army Carolina Maneuvers, and returned to Fort Benning on 3 December 1941, four days before Pearl Harbor.

On 29 December 1941 the division moved to Camp Gordon, newly built outside Augusta, Georgia, and Augusta was home for the next fifteen months. It went out to the Carolina Maneuver Area on 7 July 1942, was pulled out of the maneuvers early, and was alerted for overseas movement. Nothing came of it. The division was alerted again on Christmas Day. Nothing came of that either. The landings went into North Africa in November 1942 without the 4th, which went on rehearsing assaults in Georgia.

Two things were holding it back, and both sat far above a rifleman. The first was shipping. Between October 1942 and March 1943 not one American division left the United States, and between March and November 1943 only eleven did. The second was the experiment itself. A motorized division ate about as much cargo space as an armored division without hitting as hard, and no theater commander asked for one. On 12 March 1943 the War Department approved replacing motorized divisions with standard infantry divisions, and every motorized division except the 4th was converted that month. The 4th was held back because the Operations Division would not quite let go, keeping it as a trained nucleus for a function the value of which has not been conclusively disproved. On 16 June 1943 the European theater answered that it had no place for a motorized division in its plans for 1944. The redesignation as the 4th Infantry Division took effect on 4 August 1943, three years and two months into the experiment.

By then the division had already moved to Fort Dix, on 12 April 1943, about thirty miles and forty minutes down the road from Highland Park. If the cookout at Donaldson Park has a year, this is the likeliest one. On 19 September it went to Camp Gordon Johnston at Carrabelle, on a twenty-mile stretch of the Florida Gulf coast, to learn how to get off a landing craft. The men called it Hell-by-the-Sea. They lived in tents and huts with sand floors among the snakes and the mosquitoes, and Omar Bradley, who had taken the 28th Division through the place, wrote later that the man who selected that site should have been court-martialed for stupidity. Whatever else it was, it was the first training the division had ever done for the job it would actually do.

The division reached Fort Jackson, South Carolina, on 1 December 1943 and was alerted for overseas movement, this time in earnest. It staged from 4 January 1944 and sailed from the New York Port of Embarkation on 18 January, bound for England, aboard the Capetown Castle, the Franconia and the George Washington. The divisional chronology puts the staging at Camp Kilmer, which stood in Piscataway and Edison about two miles from Highland Park; one detailed account puts the division back at Fort Dix instead. If it was Kilmer, Joe spent his last fortnight in the United States close enough to the house to see the same sky over the Raritan.

He sailed as a private first class in Company B, 1st Battalion, 8th Infantry Regiment. He was thirty-one.

Before he left, he took Fred Sbrilli Sr. aside.

“He says, Fred, he says, ‘I’m not coming back, cause I got a tough assignment.’”

Fred Sbrilli Sr., Home News, 6 June 1994

Fred told no one, least of all his wife, for fifty years.

H Hour

Joe reached England that winter. The spring went to a single rehearsal, worked over and over on a stretch of the south Devon coast, on the English Channel, chosen because it looked like the beach they were going to. Between 22 and 30 April the assault force loaded into its ships and put itself ashore at Slapton Sands in a full-dress run called Exercise Tiger.

The practice landing went in on the 27th. Before dawn on the 28th, German E-boats found the follow-up convoy in Lyme Bay and sank two landing ships. Seven hundred and forty-nine American soldiers and sailors died, most of them engineers and quartermaster troops of the follow-up echelon. Ironically, more men died rehearsing Utah Beach than died taking it.

He wrote home to Highland Park every day that spring, the family said, and two of the letters were still in the house sixty years later when the Home News Tribune came to read them. On 18 May, with “a little time on my hands,” he told Alma about an outdoor church service and asked for Italian sausage; some of the fellows had had some sent, he wrote, and it was fine when it got there. On 22 May he asked for a bottle of Carstairs whiskey and for chewing gum, because “the kids ask you for gum over here.” He signed off:

“So long, Joe.”

Pfc. Joseph V. Ducceschi to Alma Sbrilli, 22 May 1944. The last words the family had from him.

Colonel James Van Fleet’s 8th Infantry went in first. Its 1st and 2nd Battalions were the assault force, and the first wave was twenty landing craft carrying a thirty-man assault team each: six hundred men of the 8th Infantry, ten craft for Tare Green beach and ten for Uncle Red. The touchdown of those boats at 0630 was the moment the whole VII Corps timetable was built around. The 1st Battalion, under Lieutenant Colonel Conrad C. Simmons, was to come ashore on Tare Green, opposite the strongpoint at les Dunes de Varreville. Company B was one of its rifle companies.

They came in wrong. Two of the control vessels that were to guide the wave were gone before it started, one fouled on a dan buoy and one sunk, probably by a mine; in addition, the bombardment had thrown up a wall of smoke and sand that hid the landmarks, and a strong tidal current pushed the boats south. The whole first wave touched down astride Exit 2, near la Madeleine, about two thousand yards from where it had been briefed. The Army historian who interviewed the men that summer recorded them wading the last stretch, reaching dry sand and waving their rifles: “Goddam, we’re on French soil.” German artillery put a few air bursts over the water. Otherwise there was no opposition at H Hour.

It turned out to be the luckiest mistake of the morning. The beach there was thinner in obstacles and the positions behind it weaker. Brigadier General Theodore Roosevelt Jr., the assistant division commander, had come ashore with the leading wave; he recognized the error, walked the ground under fire to find the causeways, and settled the plan on the spot with Simmons and Lieutenant Colonel Carlton O. MacNeely of the 2nd Battalion.

“We’ll start the war from right here.”

Brigadier General Theodore Roosevelt Jr., Utah Beach, 6 June 1944

What was in front of the 1st Battalion was the fortification in and around la Madeleine, field works covering the causeway road, “not formidable” in the Army’s words, taken at company strength or less against light opposition. Then the battalion turned north, went west on the causeway at Exit 3 across the flooded ground behind the dunes under artillery fire, and by evening it was near Turqueville, several miles inland.

Joe wouldn’t make it that far.

A Mile Inland

The fullest account of how Joe died is a letter. Some time after D-Day a sergeant in his unit named Stumpf wrote to Alma in Highland Park.

“(Ducceschi) did make the landing and met his death a mile from the beach. He blew up a German headquarters building with a charge of dynamite and couldn’t get away from the blast in time. Joe was always a good soldier and met his death as a hero.”

Sgt. Stumpf to Alma Sbrilli, 1944, as printed in the Home News Tribune, 30 May 2004

He added that as soon as the mess was over he would pay her a visit, and he kept his word. Fred Jr. was there for it, and sixty years on gave the paper what Stumpf had said in person: the unit had been pinned down on the beach and a pillbox had to be blown; Joe volunteered to place the charge; the fuse was shorter than he thought, and he was running for cover when it went. The blast killed him.

The family’s own telling, given to the Home News in 1994, is shorter. The Army, as they had pieced it together, took his Pennsylvania coal-country beginnings as the right background for demolition work. He came ashore carrying a load of dynamite with orders to blow up a German bunker. He did the job. The blast came too fast for him to get away. It reads like the tough assignment he had told Fred about.

The Army’s record says less: killed in action, 6 June 1944, Purple Heart. The 1st Battalion spent its first hours ashore clearing the German works in and around la Madeleine, then crossed the flooded fields on the Exit 3 causeway, and a mile from the water is well inside the ground it covered that morning.

Utah was the cheapest of the five beaches, and the Army’s own numbers say so. The official history counts 118 casualties in the 8th and 22nd Infantry together on D-Day, twelve of them killed.

Highland Park, June 1944

The call came to the house in Highland Park on a party line. Somebody else was on it and would not hang up, so the operator broke in, and Alma took the news. Fifty years later she could not say what the voice had told her.

“My God, I don’t know. All I know is I couldn’t talk because I couldn’t believe what I was hearing.”

Alma Sbrilli, Home News, 6 June 1994

Fred Jr. was a few days short of ten, and called it the saddest day of his young life. What Joe had told Fred Sr. before he sailed, Fred kept to himself. Alma heard it for the first time in 1994, in front of the reporter. The Daily Home News of New Brunswick reported Joe as the first man from the area to fall in the invasion. He had been a soldier for the better part of three years, and he was in France for one day.

The Home News would report on Joe twice: on 6 June 1994, for the fiftieth anniversary, when Alma was eighty-two and still living in Highland Park, and on 30 May 2004, for the sixtieth. The family kept both clippings. Between them they hold nearly everything on this page that a government file does not.

Citizen

Stephen Ducceschi never learned to read or write, and it cost him the one thing he wanted from the country. The citizenship test asked for literacy and he could not pass it. He grieved his son and was, the family said, fiercely proud of him; he loved America and held that serving in its army was a high honor. One thing rankled.

“He used to say he gave his son to the country and still couldn’t be a citizen.”

Fred Sbrilli Jr., Home News, 6 June 1994

When he was old he went back to Italy to die. Alma took the case to the United States ambassador in Rome, Clare Boothe Luce, who held the post from 1953 to 1956, and Luce intervened. Stephen Ducceschi, who had lost his son at Normandy, died an American.

Plot A, Row 14, Grave 12

The Army buried him first in the temporary cemetery designated Sainte-Mère-Église No. 2, which lay at Blosville, a few miles south of the town it was named for. After the war, when the American dead of the Normandy campaign were gathered in from the temporary cemeteries across the lodgement, each family chose between a grave in France and a grave at home. His chose France. He was moved to the permanent ground on the bluff at Colleville-sur-Mer, above Omaha Beach, some twenty miles east of the sand he crossed: plot A, the first of the cemetery’s ten plots, fourteenth row, twelfth grave.

The marble reads JOSEPH V. DUCCESCHI, the name in full and correct. At home he had always been Giuseppe.

In the late 1980s Fred Sbrilli Jr., the boy from the Donaldson Park cookout, went to Normandy to find the grave. He asked directions in English and got blank looks, from people he remains convinced understood every word. A local man watched this happen, turned on them in French, loudly set them straight, and sent him on to a cemetery.

“It was a cold, rainy, bitter day and I was the only person in the cemetery. There was a man in the guardhouse and he gave me general directions to my uncle’s grave.”

Fred Sbrilli Jr., Home News Tribune, 30 May 2004

“You look at those rows and rows and it tells you what awful days those were.”

Fred Sbrilli Jr., same

Telling it years later, the 2004 article has the family fixing the grave’s exact place only afterward, from a list of the burials posted online: plot A, row 14, grave 12, at Colleville. It also names the cemetery the nephew was sent to as Draguignan, which is five hundred miles south in Provence. Whether he was misdirected that far or the paper garbled the name is one for the family to settle.

The Ground

The Normandy American Cemetery stands on the site of the temporary St. Laurent cemetery, opened in the first days after the landings and the first American cemetery on French soil in the Second World War. France granted the 172.5 acres to the United States for an unlimited term, free of any charge or tax. The American Battle Monuments Commission built the permanent cemetery and dedicated it in July 1956. It holds 9,389 graves in ten plots laid out as a Latin cross, with a circular chapel at the crossing. The headstones are white Lasa marble, 9,238 crosses and 151 Stars of David, every one cut to the same pattern as his.

Roosevelt is here too. He went in with the first wave at fifty-six, the only general officer to do so, on a cane and with a bad heart, and spent the morning on his feet under fire, leading group after group over the seawall and pointing them inland, which brought him the Medal of Honor. He stayed with the division through the fighting that followed, and five weeks later, sadly, the heart gave out: in his sleeping truck at Méautis on 12 July, the same day Eisenhower approved his promotion to command the 90th Division. His brother Quentin, an aviator killed in 1918, was brought from his own war to lie beside him in 1955. Forty-five pairs of brothers are buried here, thirty of them side by side. Two of them are Preston and Robert Niland, killed a day apart, Preston with the 22nd Infantry of Joe’s own division. The Army pulled their surviving brother Fritz out of Normandy and sent him home, and that story became Saving Private Ryan. Behind the memorial, in a semicircular garden, 1,557 names are cut into the Walls of the Missing, with bronze rosettes marking the men found since.

It is the most visited of the American war cemeteries, with more than a million visitors a year, and the neighbors have taken the graves on. Les Fleurs de la Mémoire began with two men who met in September 1984, when Frank Towers, an American veteran who had come ashore at Vierville with the 30th Infantry Division a week after the landings, called at the town hall in Saint-Jean-de-Daye and a local family interpreted for him. Towers and Claude Lavieille founded the association at the end of 2000 to put flowers on the graves of the Americans who rest in Normandy. They set out to find families for five hundred graves and passed two thousand in the first year. The figure now stands above ten thousand.

The Bench

Open. The build for this dossier has yet to be chosen, and the record now offers three scenes: the jeep with its mounted machine gun at Fort Benning; the first-wave landing craft, twenty boats and thirty men each, ramps down off la Madeleine; or the flooded field behind the dunes, with a demolition party wading toward a concrete position. The record now supplies the charge, the fuse, and a sergeant’s word for the target. The studio’s call.

Debrief

The official file is spare: nine digits, a regiment, a date, and a line in a casualty tally on the cheapest beach of the five, on a day the histories call a walkover. The rest of him lived outside the file, in the town, the family, and the paper that came back to him twice.

What holds him is two yellowed clippings and a family’s memory. He was thirty-one, a grown man who had helped keep a household afloat since he was a teenager. Stephen the dishwasher’s calm younger son. Alma’s kid brother, two years apart and always close. The uncle who made a boy’s best day out of hot dogs and baked potatoes in Donaldson Park. A soldier who was in the Army before Pearl Harbor and ashore in the first hour of the first morning; who told his brother-in-law he had a tough assignment and was not coming back, and then wrote home every day that spring about church services, sausage and gum, and signed off So long, Joe. He went a mile inland with the dynamite and did the job. His sister took the call. A sergeant wrote to her that he had died a hero, and came to Highland Park after the war to say it to her face. His father, who could not read the country’s citizenship test, gave it his son and died an American. His nephew went and stood at the grave in the rain. Strangers in Normandy bring the flowers now.

Giuseppe, to the people who loved him. Joe to everyone else.
