Esilda Ducceschi Zini · 1875–1944

The Hills Above Pistoia

Joseph Ducceschi’s father, Stephen, came out of the Tuscan Apennine above Pistoia. Calamecca is one of those villages, set high on the mountain in what was then the comune of Piteglio, six hundred and forty metres up, with the Pescia torrent running below it. Esilda Ducceschi was born there on 18 June 1875 and married Vittorio Zini. By the autumn of 1944 she was sixty-nine and he was eighty-four.

The Order

In the late summer of 1944 the front was climbing the mountain. The German armies were falling back from Rome toward the Linea Gotica, the Gothic Line drawn across the Apennine, and the villages of the Pistoian mountains sat directly in the path of the retreat. On 3 September an order was posted in the square at Calamecca: the village was to be evacuated, and anyone who ignored it would be shot. Der Räumungsbefehl — the evacuation order.

Most of Calamecca went down to the plain or into the woods. The people who stayed were the old and the infirm, and the families who would not leave them. Vittorio Zini was eighty-four and could not be moved far. Esilda stayed with him.

The Pescia

On 19 September an SS patrol came into the village and worked through the houses, and then down to the shelters along the bed of the Pescia. Esilda and Vittorio were shot there together, alongside a neighbor, Rosa Finocchi. Rosa’s husband was wounded and lived only by lying still among the dead until the patrol had gone.

Fourteen villagers were killed at Calamecca in those September days, five of them women and two of them children — the Pocci brothers, Oscar and Silvano, fourteen and eleven. There was no military object in it. A retreating army was clearing the ground behind itself: die Vergeltungsmaßnahme, the reprisal, in its plainest form. The Italian state’s catalogue of the massacres, the Atlante delle stragi naziste e fasciste in Italia, carries every name, and records the men who did it as unknown.

The Stone

The village cut a stone for the first anniversary and stood it in Piazza Francesco Ferrucci, raised by the people of Calamecca under the auspices of the Socialists and the Communists both. Its language is the high sacrificial idiom of 1945.

On the days of 19 and 20 September 1944, nazi-fascist troops, falling back up the Apennine, beaten by the Allied and partisan forces, destroying homes and belongings, after unspeakable iniquities, barbarously killed thirteen innocent villagers — an elect company of men, women and children, guilty only of hating the invader and of loving homeland, family and labor.

Memorial stone, Piazza Francesco Ferrucci, Calamecca, first anniversary, September 1945. Translated from the Italian; the roster of names is left as carved.

The stone names thirteen; the Atlante counts fourteen. The one it leaves off is Luisa Luciani, an outsider, a displaced woman killed along the Pescia on the 22nd and not of the village. Two more women, taken to the barracks at Macchia Antonini, are remembered on a separate stone there. Esilda is among the thirteen the village named as its own.

He Wrote Home Every Day

PFC Joseph Ducceschi, Stephen’s son, was in the Army before Pearl Harbor and ashore in the first hour at Utah Beach. He was killed on 6 June 1944, fifteen weeks before the patrol came down to the Pescia.
