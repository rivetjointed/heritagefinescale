Dossier No. 001

The Stuka Never Mentioned

Junkers Ju 87 B-2 "Picchiatello": German paint under Italian markings, proving its effectiveness across the Axis air forces.

Subject Data

Subject
Junkers Ju 87 B-2 · MM.7085 · tactical "239"
Service
239ª Squadriglia, 97° Gruppo · Regia Aeronautica · Cap. Giuseppe Cenni, CO · pilot + rear gunner/radio
Theater
Mediterranean & Balkans · anti-shipping and ground attack · 1940–43
Debrief
The blitzkrieg icon presented in its Axis partner's air force

Aircraft

Field Note

Picchiatello: From picchiata, the Italian word for the dive. Roughly "the little diver," with an affectionate edge: the same word can mean a bit mad. Italian crews named the aircraft for the thing it did.

The Junkers Ju 87 needs no introduction. But that is precisely the problem. The inverted gull wing, the fixed spatted gear, the wailing siren: the Stuka is among the most instantly recognized silhouettes of the early war, welded in memory to the Wehrmacht's 1939–40 blitzkrieg in Poland, France, and the Low Countries. What the popular record largely bypasses is that Italy requested roughly one hundred of them from the senior partner in the Pact of Steel, its own aviation industry having failed to produce a serviceable dive-bomber. Deliveries began in the summer of 1940 (fifty-two B-2s, then fifty longer-ranged R-2s), and importing a proven German design closed that gap far faster than any domestic program was capable of at that time; the Regia Aeronautica's Stuka strength ultimately reached some 159 airframes of all marks, the D-3s of 1943 included.

This airframe, Matricola Militare 7085, comes from that first consignment. The subject is Italeri's 1:48 kit No. 2769, detailed with option A: tactical number 239. The markings are those of Capitano Giuseppe Cenni, 239ª Squadriglia, Balkan front (Yugoslavia/Greece), out of Albania, April 1941.

Mission

Field Note

R-2: The longer-ranged R-2 traded bomb load for fuel, tuned for the open-sea anti-shipping role; the B-2 was the shorter-legged dive bomber. Italy flew both.

Italy entered the war in June 1940 lacking a usable, effective dive bomber. Its own domestic attempt, the Savoia-Marchetti SM.85, proved a documented fiasco. The solution was bought, not built: ex-Luftwaffe Ju 87s equipped the Regia Aeronautica's 96° Gruppo Bombardamento a Tuffo from the summer of 1940, the 97° Gruppo (whose 238ª and 239ª Squadriglie included this aircraft) later that year, and in 1941 the 101° and 102° Gruppi; with the D-3s of 1943, the fleet reached some 159 of all marks.

The Picchiatelli flew where the Mediterranean war was hardest: against Malta, against Greece and Yugoslavia from the Puglian airfields, against Allied shipping and ground targets in North Africa and the Balkans. The dive bomber Italy could not build became the workhorse of the missions Italy could not otherwise fly.

Crew

Field Note

Stukaschule, Graz: Italy's dive-bomber crews were trained at the Luftwaffe's own school in Austria. The aircraft were not the only German transfer; the doctrine came with them.

Giuseppe Cenni was twenty-five when he took command of the 239ª Squadriglia in November 1940, already a Spanish Civil War fighter ace with eight victories, and a former prisoner of war: a mid-air collision in fog forced him to bail out over Spain, and he spent seven harsh months in captivity before a prisoner exchange freed him. An aerobatic instructor as well, he was sent through the Luftwaffe's own dive-bombing school at Graz, Austria, about ninety miles southwest of Vienna. From Lecce-Galatina in Puglia he led the squadriglia through the Greek and Yugoslav campaigns, then to North Africa and the convoy war.

Over the Aegean in 1941 he developed a ricochet-style attack the Italians widely adopted for its effectiveness: release low and flat, bounce the bomb off the sea, and let it strike the target ship at the waterline. It was skip bombing, arrived at independently and proven in combat well before the Allies made the technique famous in the Pacific. Cenni would rise to Maggiore, command the 102° Gruppo, and convert his men to the Reggiane Re.2002. Ordered on 3 September 1943, five days before the armistice, to attack the Reggio Calabria landing (Operation Baytown), he was killed the next day. He was twenty-eight. Italy awarded him, posthumously, its Gold Medal of Military Valor; Germany had already given him the Iron Cross.

The second seat of a Picchiatello held a gunner/radio operator facing aft, names rarely preserved where the pilots' are. The brief notes the asymmetry rather than omitting it, much as the name itself was.

Scheme

Field Note

Quellenkritik: The overpaint is not conjecture. Period photographs show Regia Aeronautica markings laid directly over Luftwaffe insignia, the German scheme beneath them unchanged.

Here is the detail that makes this airframe worth a dossier, and it is documented, not modeler's license: the first Italian Ju 87s were not factory-fresh deliveries. They were drawn from Luftwaffe stocks, and the German insignia were simply overpainted and replaced with Regia Aeronautica markings. The aircraft kept their factory RLM scheme (Schwarzgrün RLM 70 and Dunkelgrün RLM 71 splinter over Hellblau RLM 65 undersides) while their nationality was painted over, literally, at the unit level.

This build reconstructs that sequence physically rather than depicting only its end state. The Luftwaffe markings went on first, as the airframe would have left German hands. They were then overpainted, and the Italian insignia applied above them: the white Savoy cross on the tail, the fasces discs under the wings, the white Mediterranean theater band, the yellow cowling of the Axis Mediterranean recognition scheme. Under the Italian paint of the finished model, the German layer is still there: the same stratigraphy the real airframes carried.

The build record

Debrief

Field Note

"Valzer, ragazzi!" Cenni's radio call to start an attack: Waltz, boys! Since 1993 the 102° Gruppo has carried it on the tails of its aircraft.

The Stuka's wider Axis usage is overshadowed by its dramatic early Blitzkrieg sorties. Not falsely (its German service is the reason anyone recognizes it) but incompletely, in a way that flattens what the aircraft actually was. By 1941 it was no longer the spearhead of a winning doctrine, but a hand-me-down propping up an ally whose own industry had failed to deliver. The hundred-odd airframes Germany passed to Italy did for the Regia Aeronautica what Lend-Lease aircraft did for the Allies (kept a flagging effort flying), and that half of the supply story is almost never told, because dependency is a worse legend than blitzkrieg.

This model argues the fuller record in paint. The German markings under the Italian ones are not artistic license; they are the documented history of these specific airframes, which left Luftwaffe stocks with their insignia overpainted at unit level. The Stuka is infamous as the screaming icon of the 1940 Blitzkrieg. The operational record, however, includes MM.7085: German-built, German-painted, Italian-flown, led by a twenty-five-year-old who learned the trade at a German school and taught a tactic the textbooks would later credit to others. What gets remembered and what actually happened are, as usual, two different paint layers. You just have to know the second one is still underneath.
